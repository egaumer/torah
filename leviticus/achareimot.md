Yom Kippur, or The Day of Atonement, gives us the necessary backdrop for understanding the scope of what Jesus paid for our sins. 

This holy day falls in autumn, sometime in September or October. On the Jewish calendar, it is the 10th day of the 7th month. (Did you catch those numbers? Do they have meaning that you know of?) 10= _____ 7=____. The number ten is the number of law and order (10 Commandments—Exodus 20), completeness and human responsibility (ten plagues—Exodus 7-12), and tithing (Leviticus 27:30 and Deuteronomy 14:22). The number seven is the number of creation (order out of chaos—Genesis 1-2), the Sabbath (day of rest and worship—Exodus 20:8-11), completeness and perfection (Revelation 1-3), and covenants and promises (Genesis 9:12-17, Noah’s rainbow of seven colors). 

This day is a solemn holiday, one of fasting and repentance of sins. Yom Kippur was not the only fasting day in Judaism but the only fast mandated by Scripture. It was also a day that prohibited all forms of work. It was so important that if an Israelite did not devote themselves to the rules, they were “cut off from the people” (Lev. 23:29). Again, we look at the words “cut off,” and it doesn’t have that much meaning—maybe we think of being cut out of an inheritance. However, in that culture, being “cut off” means being excommunicated from the entire community. It was also a form of spiritual separation, and, in some cases, it led to the death penalty (Lev. 23:30). When God used this term, it was very, very serious.

# Day 2 &middot; _The Scapegoat_

Let me just back up a bit, and let’s talk about the Hebrew calendar and the day of Yom Kippur. It is held on the ___th day of the ____ month. Remember, numbers are significant in the Bible. Seven Biblical feasts take place over seven months. The first month brings us Passover, the Feast of Unleavened Bread, and the First Fruits. (We will learn more from Karen Seddon when we get to Leviticus 23!) The seventh month is a Sabbath month and brings us Rosh Hashanah (Jewish New Year), then ten days later Yom Kippur, and five days later Sukkot. Yom Kippur is the 10th day of the 7th month. Both numbers 7 and 10 are the biblical numbers of completion (in the sense of fullness, not something ending). 

The most dramatic shift in how Yom Kippur was observed happened in 70 AD when the Romans destroyed the Temple. The city was rebuilt, but the Temple never was. All of the rituals we’ve been studying in Leviticus, including the following ones, required the Temple and altars. In Genesis 9:4, God placed some rules on meat as food. While man could eat the flesh, they could not eat the blood because life is in the blood. God made blood unsuitable for food. Blood was dedicated solely for atonement. The blood was sacred. 

Any person would understand that if you cut yourself deep enough, you would bleed out and die. Life is in the blood. Plus, there is a pattern of misuse of blood: murder ended a life (shedding blood), and drinking or eating the blood of an animal was misuse because it was for atonement, not sustenance. Sacrificing an animal to another god was misused because a living creature that our Holy God created belonged to Him. 

Leviticus 17 emphasizes regulations for the proper place for offering sacrifices. Verse 3 explicitly calls for the centralization of worship, instructing that sacrifices should occur at the Tabernacle rather than in various locations. 

This underscores the importance of a unified worship practice. The idea is to prevent people from engaging in practices that might lead to idolatry or compromise the central authority of God’s commands. 

God wanted worship to be corporate, together, and centralized in one place. He knew the tendency would be for the Israelites to follow other people's worship systems. An Israelite couldn’t just say, “Hey, it’s a nice day to be in the desert with my friends, racing camels. How about if we do a campfire out there? Plus, I’m not a fan of the worship songs in the Tabernacle, and I have to get up early.” Nope. God wants His worship to be corporate and centralized.

Can you have church at a camp in the mountains or on the golf course? Sure, but that’s not enough. We need to be together. We need God’s people. We need other believers around us. God created us to be social beings.
