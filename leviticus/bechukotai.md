# _Bechukotai_ &sdot; בְּחֻקֹּתַי &sdot; Leviticus 26:3-27

The last reading from the book of Leviticus is called Bechukotai, which means “in My statutes.” The name comes from the first verse of the reading.

```
Leviticus 26:3, “If you ____ my decrees and are ____ to ___ my commands…”
```

This last portion of Leviticus promises blessings and rewards for Israel if they keep the Torah, but punishment and curses if they break the commands of the Torah. Yikes! 

When you read that first verse again, doesn’t it sound redundant? “Follow my decrees” and “obey my commands”? Think of it this way: following God’s decrees is studying God’s word, specifically the Torah. Keeping the commands refers to how they are acted out. We should learn God’s word to do it.

If. It’s the biggest little word there is. The history of Israel cannot be adequately understood apart from the “ifs” in God’s covenant. It also underscores our relationship with God. 

* Obedience is the foundation of blessing.
* These final two chapters focus on the responsibilities we have as Christians.

The word “if” appears eight times in this chapter. The words “I will” appear a staggering 28 times. We will look at promises God makes that are conditional on obedience. 

If you do this, I’ll do that. If you don’t do this, I will do that. There are consequences. This is a part of life. My parents gave me those parameters all the time. That’s how I learned. God gives us four reasons why we should obey the Lord: because of 1) who God is (vs.1), 2) what God did (vs 13), where God dwells (vs. 12), and 4) what God promised (vs. 4-12).

This chapter begins with a powerful call to worship. God instructs His people to have no other gods before Him, to make no idols, and to honor the Sabbath. We’ve heard God say this over and over again. This is one of those “repeat to remember” things. God is repeating Himself so the people remember. Why? Because the people had other gods and did not observe the Sabbath. 

```
The last sentence of verse 1 says, “I am the ___ your ___.”

The last sentence of verse 2 says, “I am the ___.”
```

We’ve talked about this before, but did you notice anything different about the spelling of the words in these sentences? The word LORD is all capitals. 

The Bible’s term ‘lord’ (all lowercase)  often refers to a person of authority or a master. It calls us to respect authority while understanding its limitations. 

When we see ‘Lord’ with an initial capital, it is Elohiym or Adonai in Hebrew. This symbol shows the Almighty's divine authority and sovereignty. 

But when we see ‘LORD’ in all uppercase letters, it is the divine name of Yahweh (YHWH), or Jehovah. This is God’s personal and intimate name. It refers to the unchanging God, who was and is and is to come. 

```
Look at Psalm 8:1 and fill in the correct spelling of “lord.” “Oh ___, our ___, how majestic is your name in all the earth.”
```

This verse is saying, “Oh LORD (our eternal, unchanging God, who was and is and is to come), our Lord (mater, and sovereign ruler), how majestic is your name in all the earth.” 

See the difference? (Now you’ll notice when “lord” is spelled in all lowercase, one capital, or all caps. You’re welcome!)

I’m going to separate Leviticus 26 into two parts. The first is all about the blessings of the obedient. Part 2, tomorrow, will be about the curses of being disobedient. Either way you slice it, our responses to God’s commands always bring consequences. There is no neutral ground. Obedience has positive consequences (called “blessings” and life with God), and disobedience has negative consequences (called “curses” and separation from God).

3—Where God dwells: God dwelt with the people in the Tabernacle. His spirit was with them as they faced dangers. And guess what? He lives in you! 

4- What God promised. Let’s see what that is…

Rain was extremely significant—when it occurred and how much. We can all think of times when rains and floods caused incredible damage to life and property. In verse 5, God is telling them that He will water the land so that the land opens up its harvest fully for the people. Not only will they have enough food, but they will also be safe in the land. What a promise! 

God is going to cause blessings to flow actively. This is not passive. He didn’t say, “I will allow it.” God will actively cause the conditions in which the people will have full bellies and peace in the land. Vows are voluntary. No one is required to make a vow. Ananias and Sapphira did not have to vow their land to the Lord. But they did. And when they held some back, God killed them. 

The vows found here are for that person’s service to the sanctuary…for the dedication of a person to the LORD.  In this vow, a person may want to serve in the church but couldn't because they were not a Levite. So they would vow service and then redeem it with money. It was a way to donate. It sounds weird, but that’s how they did it. We see examples like that in people like Samson and Samuel. They vowed service to the Lord in the form of a Nazirite vow. They promised to serve as a judge or prophet…being dedicated to God. This is not because men are more valuable than women. It’s simply because men are more suited for heavy work, heavy lifting in the tabernacle. This is what we are dealing with: a person dedicated to the Lord’s work.

Verses 9-13 talk about redeeming pledged animals. If an animal was a part of the vow given to the Lord, here were the rules. Verse 12 gives us insight into who sets the prices for the animals. In those days, people could mint their own coins. So, the priests would weigh their coins so they would pay the correct prices. When the first Temple was built, the priests minted temple counts. There was a caveat—if the animal was designated as a holy animal, set apart for the LORD, there could be no substitution. Once it was deemed holy to the LORD, it became God’s holy property, and the penalty for taking it was death. As soon as the person determined which of his animals he would offer (and it had to be with no blemishes), then it was a done deal.

# Day 4 &middot; _The Heart of Tithing_

<span style="font-size: 1.25em"><i class="fa-solid fa-book-open"></i>&nbsp; **Read** |</span> Leviticus 27:14-25.

&nbsp;

```
Leviticus 27:14 “‘If anyone dedicates their ____  as something holy to the Lord, the priest will judge its quality as good or bad.”
```

Do you remember where the Israelites are right now? They are in the wilderness at the base of Mount Sinai. And do you remember how long it’s been since God parted the Red Sea and freed them from the bondage of Pharaoh? Less than a year. Less than a year ago, they packed their belongings and left Egypt. 

They do not have houses, family land, or fields right now. And they won’t have them for another 40 years. God is providing for them and giving them hope for a future. He is not saying IF you have a house or land…He’s saying, “If you decide to dedicate your houses.” 

God is giving them a foreshadowing that they will someday be in a place where a freewill offering will be a house or their land. 

Why would someone give an animal, land, or a house to God when they would turn around and redeem it at a higher price? Remember, we’re dealing with an ancient culture that was farming-based. It was a way to volunteer your services to the Tabernacle if you were not a Levite. But it also may have been that the person didn’t have enough money then, so the Tabernacle held it for them as collateral. 

The option for redemption was always available. If someone wanted to reclaim what was dedicated, they could add a fifth to its value. Redemption comes at a cost. 
All of these scriptures should remind us that, ultimately, all our possessions are God’s. We are just stewards, not owners. 

Verses 30-33 speak of tithes: one of the crops from the land and one of the animals. 

```
Leviticus 27:30: “A ___ of everything from the land, whether grain from the soil or fruit from the trees, belongs to the ___; it is ___ to the Lord.”
```

Tithing is an acknowledgment that everything we have ultimately belongs to God. The land, the crops, the herds…God is the ultimate owner and provider. Tithing is a tangible way of recognizing and affirming God’s ownership in our lives. 

Whenever the topic of tithing comes up, we all get a little nervous. This particular tithe was cut and dry: 10%. God requires a 1/10th of the person’s increase—whatever that may be. 
This was not the only tithe. Remember that the people also gave the first fruits of their herds and crops. A third tithe was also to be given every three years. 

Tithing isn’t just a financial obligation; it’s an act of consecration. By setting aside a portion for God, we declare that our resources are set apart for God’s purposes. It’s our way of saying, “Lord, this belongs to You, and I honor Your holiness in my giving.” 

So, here’s the burning question, “Should we tithe based on net or gross? Before or after taxes?” 

Well, the New Testament says this: 

```
Luke 6:38: “Give, and it will be given to ___. A good measure, pressed down, shaken together and running over, will be ___  into your lap. For with the ___ you use, it will be ____ to you.”
```

```
2 Corinthians 9:6-7 “The point is this: whoever sows ___ will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows _____ will also reap bountifully.  Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a ____ giver.”
```

God requires 10%. We should give because we are happy to be blessed, that God is good, and we have a cheerful heart. Use the two verses above to help you know how to tithe to God’s work. Our tithe is about consistency and a deliberate commitment to honor God with our first fruits. It’s about supporting the ministry of the Church, caring for those in need, and advancing the kingdom of God. 

When we pray to ask God to give something to us, do we negotiate with Him on how much He should provide us? Or do we want God to give to us bountifully? Then why do we worry about net or gross? Give cheerfully. Give because you love the Lord. Give because His work is going to get done, and His Kingdom is going to expand. Give like you would like to receive. 

The primary lesson in this chapter is that God expects us to keep our commitments to Him and to be honest in our dealings with Him. 

Jesus Christ paid—with His own life—the redemption price for sinners, and we were not worth it! Any sacrifice we make for Him is nothing compared to the sacrifice He made for us. #perspective

# Day 5 &middot; _Highlights_

We are at the end of our journey through the book of Leviticus! You did it! Here, we find ourselves at the intersection of ancient rituals and eternal truths. 

Leviticus is one of those books that needs to be studied, not just read, for us to understand. 

Let’s look at some of the highlights of Leviticus. 
* It is the only book in the Bible that discusses holiness.
* It describes the requirements for fellowship with God.
* It establishes the grounds for fellowship with God: sacrifice.
* Each of the sacrifices is a shadow of Jesus, the Messiah.
* We are required to be separate.
* God’s calendar is not only commemorative but prophetic. 

There are many groups of seven in this book: 
* Seven days in a week: Shabbat, The Sabbath
* Seven weeks: Feast of Weeks
* Seven months: Feast of Tabernacles
* Seven Years: Sabbatical Year/ Year of Jubilee

Here’s another bit of fun trivia—there are 70 specifically appointed times for holy days called “mo’ed.” There are 52 Sabbaths, seven days of Passover, one day for Shavuot (Feast of Pentecost), one day for the Feast of Trumpets, one day for Yom Kippur (Day of Atonement), seven days of Sukkot (Feast of Tabernacles), and one day for the eighth day of Assembly after Sukkot. 

The first three feasts, the Spring feasts, have already been fulfilled in the first coming of Christ. The last three feasts, the Fall feasts, are prophetic of the second coming of Christ. 

According to Chuck Missler in his book on Leviticus, he summarizes Leviticus in 10 ways: 

1- Our God is a Holy God. Whenever we minimize the holiness of God, we are in danger of reducing human sinfulness and minimizing the world of Christ on the cross. God’s holiness means His complete “apartness” from anything sinful. 

2—Our God wants His people to be Holy. He says, “Be holy, for I am holy.” He calls His people a “holy nation.” 

3—Holiness Begins at the Altar. Leviticus didn’t begin with a prayer meeting. It started at the altar, where innocent sacrifices shed their blood for guilty sinners. We can measure our holiness by our recognition and hatred of sin. 

4—Holiness Involves Obedience and Discipline. Our holiness wasn’t completed by bringing a sacrifice to the altar and walking away unchanged. It was designed to shock us into the fact that sin is ugly and has a price. 

5—Holiness must come from God to be genuine. Nadab and Abihu violated God's holy law. God’s Word is the ultimate measure of holiness, not our feelings, knowledge, or religious zeal. 

6- We come to God only through our High Priest and Lamb of God, Jesus Christ (1 Peter 2:5). 

7- Lack of Holiness Affects Our Land. The welfare of a nation depends on its holiness. Idolatry and sexual immorality are polluting our land and are very contagious. 

8- Holiness—Holiness is Not a Private Affair. We are part of a worshiping community. Today's danger is the idea of “individual Christianity,” which states that each believer can be a lone ranger of holiness. We need each other. 

9- Holiness Glorifies the Lord. Only God can make a person holy- so our godly lives are a tribute to His power and grace. When people see Christ in you, give God the glory. 

10- Holiness Means Living to Please God Alone. We should live to please God alone and not to impress other people. We are His workmanship. 

The Torah is essential. To fully grasp the New Testament, we must understand the Old Testament. Leviticus is not an obsolete relic but a living testament to God’s faithfulness, justice, and grace. Studying it equips us to see the unfolding narrative of redemption in Christ. 

If you look carefully, you will see sacred threads woven through this ancient tapestry that connects us to the redemptive work of Jesus Christ. These threads of Jesus are woven throughout all of the scriptures. 

***Extra Notes***

Here are my takeaways from each chapter. What are yours? 

Leviticus 1: Worship is costly—the animal to be sacrificed needed to be the best, without blemish.  Worship is personal. It required active engagement. The burnt offering was to get God’s attention and bring peace between God and man. But atonement only covers sin; it doesn’t forgive it. The burnt offering is a foreshadowing of Christ’s sacrificial death. It was about reconciliation. 

Leviticus 2: The grain offering was voluntary and showed us that all we have (our abilities, health, wealth, jobs, hard work…) belongs to God. It was about being thankful for God’s provision and showing dedication. 

Leviticus 3: The peace offering was about fellowshipping with other believers to celebrate the peace between you and God. 

Leviticus 4: The sin offering was for forgiveness of unintentional sin. Sin has side effects; it destroys our relationship with God. 

Leviticus 5: The guilt offering is for the sin of omission. You also pay restitution as a reparation if you hurt someone else. This offering restores us to God. 

Leviticus 6: The fire on the Brazen Altar was never to go out. In the same way, the Holy Spirit fire is placed in every believer; don’t quench it. 

Leviticus 7: God repeats his instructions about the various offerings. Why repeat? Because God is a good teacher. He is interested in details and precision. It underscores the importance of obedience. 

Leviticus 8: This chapter brings us to the beginning of our story as a royal priesthood!  And God is interested in our obedience and active participation in worship. As believers, we are clothed in the righteousness of Christ—just as the priests were dressed in their holy garments. 

Leviticus 9: Our only approach to God is through sacrifice. We are set apart to be holy. God lit the altar with His heavenly fire, and our job is to keep it burning. 

Leviticus 10: Aaron’s two sons are killed when they don’t follow God’s rules by adding strange fire to God. Approaching God is a serious matter. He is holy. Again, we are reminded that worship is based on sacrifice and obedience. 

Leviticus 11 introduces us to the laws of diet: the kosher diet. God even cares about what we eat. Unclean things can pollute clean things, and the world can contaminate us. 

Leviticus 12: God will lay down some rules on purification after childbirth. These rules will directly affect Mary and Joseph as they bring their infant, Jesus Christ, to be circumcised and then brought to the Temple priest as their firstborn. 

Leviticus 13: Clean and unclean is not only about what you eat; it’s about you, personally. Skin diseases were highly contagious. Leprosy was a lot like sin. It starts small but spreads quickly and can lead to death. 

Leviticus 14: Even with the danger of skin diseases, there was a way to be cleansed. We, too, can become clean and acceptable to Yahweh once again!  The priests would perform a purification right using blood and water. Then, just as kings, prophets, and priests were anointed, the leper was also anointed with oil. 

Leviticus 15: This chapter tells us that God is concerned about our private lives. Sin is gross and infects everything we touch. But even with our filth, Jesus can cleanse us entirely and restore us to Him. Christ’s holiness also reaches to everything it touches; we must be willing to reach out and take hold of Him.

Leviticus 16: Yom Kippur, The Day of Atonement, is the highest holy day of the Jewish year. This was the day the high priest went behind the veil to the Holy of Holies. It was a day of atonement. Atonement means that their sins were covered, not taken away. Jesus will forgive and take away our sins, not just cover them! This is also where we learn about the scapegoat! 

Leviticus 17: Life is in the blood. God wanted a unified worship. Worship was to be corporate, together, and centralized. We need each other. 

Leviticus 18: This chapter outlines God’s moral laws for the Israelites, specifically addressing issues related to sexual conduct. Why? Because the family unit is essential. 

Leviticus 19: This chapter is a comprehensive guide to holy living, covering diverse aspects of ethical behavior, social justice, and religious devotion. God is holy and wants His people to be holy (set apart). 

Leviticus 20: Chapter 20 underscores the severe consequences of violating God’s moral laws, mainly focusing on penalties for engaging in idolatry, practicing occult activities, and committing various sexual sins. Why? Because God knew we would sin and wanted to save us from the devastating results of sin. 

Leviticus 21: “I am the LORD.” God repeated this four times in this chapter. God is going to set higher standards for the priests. 

Leviticus 22: “I am the LORD who makes them holy.” Wow! God repeats this nine times in this chapter! Our physical purity matters to God. He emphasizes keeping His commandments and not profaning His name. 

Leviticus 23: The Spring appointed festivals—God loves a good party! These festivals are rehearsals for a more significant event to come! 

Leviticus teaches us the importance of maintaining sacred spaces and the holiness of God’s name. Our speech and actions should reflect God’s holiness. 

Leviticus 25: God brings rest and restoration with the land, having a Sabbath year and the Year of Jubilee! 

Leviticus 26: God reminds us that there are blessings for the obedient and curses for disobedience. Our response to God’s commands always brings good or bad consequences. 
There is no neutral. 

Leviticus 27: God takes making a vow to Him very seriously. Tithing is how we recognize and affirm God’s ownership in our lives. God loves a cheerful giver!
